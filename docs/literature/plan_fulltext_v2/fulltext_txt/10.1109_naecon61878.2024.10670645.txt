Tracking a Plane Wave Direction using Sparse
Arrays with an Extended Kalman Filter
Showrov Rahman
University of Rhode Island
Kingston, RI 02881 USA
showrov rahman@uri.edu
Kaushallya Adhikari
University of Rhode Island
Kingston, RI 02881 USA
kadhikari@uri.edu
Abstract—This work focuses on estimating the direction of
arrival of a plane wave from a moving source using time domain
snapshots captured by a linear array. We employ an extended
Kalman ﬁlter by linearizing the time domain measurement
model. We consider the performance of a full array, which
serves as a benchmark for comparison with three popular sparse
arrays: coprime, nested, and shift-invariant sparse arrays. The
motivation for using such sparse arrays is to reduce overall cost,
sensor count, and computation burden. The simulation results
demonstrate that sparse arrays achieve comparable accuracy
to full arrays of equivalent aperture. Among the sparse arrays
studied, the coprime array exhibits the highest performance,
with a mean squared error closest to that of the full array.
Index Terms—Coprime array, direction of arrival (DOA), ex-
tended Kalman ﬁlter(EKF), nested array, shift-invariant sparse
array (SISA), sparse arrays
I. INTRODUCTION
A uniform linear array (ULA) is the most commonly
studied array structure for estimating the direction of arrival
(DOA) of plane waves [1]–[4]. In a ULA, sensors are posi-
tioned along a straight line with an intersensor distance of
λ/2, where λ is the wavelength of the impinging waves [1].
When intersensor spacing exceeds λ/2, aliasing artifacts may
occur. However, careful design of sparse arrays can mitigate
these artifacts. Arrays with an average intersensor spacing
greater than λ/2 are classiﬁed as sparse arrays. For this
study, we focus exclusively on sparse arrays arranged on a
uniform λ/2 grid along a line. These sparse linear arrays
(SLAs) allow for a reduction in the number of sensors by
increasing the spacing. Consequently, SLAs offer lower costs
and require less data computation compared to ULAs [5], [6].
Additionally, the sparse positioning of sensors in SLAs results
in lower mutual coupling effects [7], [8]. These advantages
make sparse arrays desirable for various applications [9]–[12],
and they come in different types [2], [13], [14].
Coprime and nested arrays are constructed by interleaving
two undersampled ULAs (ULAs with an intersensor spacing
greater than λ/2) [7], [15]. The design parameters for these
arrays include sampling factors, number of sensors, and array
lengths. By adjusting these parameters, various types of sparse
linear arrays (SLAs) can be created. A recent addition to
the category of sparse arrays is the shift-invariant sparse
array (SISA), which encompasses both coprime and nested
arrays [16]–[18]. In this study, we analyze and compare the
performance of three types of sparse arrays: (1) coprime, (2)
nested, and (3) SISA.
For signal estimation and tracking, we apply the extended
Kalman ﬁlter (EKF). The Kalman ﬁlter, introduced by R.E.
Kalman in 1960, stands as one of the most prominent and
widely used signal parameter estimators [19]. The Kalman
ﬁlter is a sequential minimum mean squared error (MMSE)
estimator and it allows the unknown parameters to evolve
in time via a dynamical model [20], [21]. A Kalman ﬁlter
can be regarded as a generalization of the Wiener ﬁlter
since a Kalman ﬁlter does not require the signals to be
stationary and scalar. Kalman ﬁlter provides the estimates of
the parameters of interest by incorporating data from past
and present [20]. When the signal and noise follow a jointly
Gaussian distribution, the Kalman ﬁlter represents the optimal
MMSE estimator. Even in scenarios where this assumption
is not met, it remains the optimal linear MMSE (LMMSE)
estimator. However, the Kalman ﬁlter is inherently limited to
linear signal and measurement models, a constraint not met
by many practical systems. In such cases, an extended version
(i.e., EKF), is employed. The EKF leverages a ﬁrst-order
Taylor series approximation to linearize non-linear signal and
measurement models, thus extending the applicability of the
traditional Kalman ﬁlter concept [21], [22]. In this study, we
utilize time domain snapshots received by a linear array as
measurements. Since these measurements are non-linear in
the parameter of interest, we apply an EKF for direction of
arrival (DOA) estimation.
This paper conducts a comparative analysis of SLAs em-
ploying an EKF for tracking the DOA of a plane wave. The
study centers on three distinct SLAs (coprime, nested, and
SISA), each with an identical number of sensors (6) and
aperture. A full ULA comprising 10 sensors is the benchmark
for comparison.
The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Section II de-
scribes the model of the signal and the estimator (EKF). Sec-
tion III explains and illustrates the array models . Section IV
presents and discusses the simulation results. Section V sums
up the study.

III. SPARSE ARRAYS
The full ULA considered in this work consists of 10 sensors
positioned from 0 to 9, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Each sparse
array considered in this study comprises 6 sensors and spans
the same aperture as a full ULA with 10 sensors. We provide
a brief description of the sparse arrays next.
A. Coprime Array
A linear coprime array is formed by interleaving two linear
subarrays whose undersampling factors are coprime integers

[6], [15], [23]–[25]. We denote the two subarrays as S1c and
S2c, respectively. The subarray S1c has a total of Me sensors
and an undersampling factor of N, while S2c has Ne sensors
and an undersampling factor of M; and M and N are coprime
integers. We set N = 3 and M = 4, Me = 4, and Ne = 3.
The sensor positions of the coprime array are shown in Fig.
1.
B. Nested Array
A linear nested array is also formed by interleaving two
subarrays. In a nested array, the ﬁrst subarray S1n has a sam-
pling factor of 1 and comprises M sensors, while the second
subarray S2n consists of N sensors with an undersampling
factor of M [6], [26]. The sensor positions of a nested array
are also illustrated in Fig. 1.
C. Shift-invariant Sparse Array (SISA)
A shift-invariant sparse array is an array where one sub-
array can be obtained by applying a spatial shift to another
subarray. The two subarrays may partially overlap and the
union of the two subarrays is the overall SISA. As illustarted
in Fig.1, the ﬁrst subarray consists of the sensors at ψ1= [0
1 4]; and applying a spatial shift of υ = 5 yields the second
subarray with the sensors at ψ2= [5 6 9].
IV. SIMULATION RESULTS
We applied the EKF to track the direction cosine of a plane
wave using the data measured by the full ULA and the sparse
arrays described in Section III. In all experiments, we used
ω0 = 0.6π, N = 200, A = 0.95, Q = 0.01, C[n] = 2I,
ˆs[−1| −1] = E{s[n]} = 0.5, and M[−1| −1] = 100.
Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, and Fig. 5 illustrate the simulation
results for the ULA, coprime array, nested array, and SISA,
respectively. The top panels in Fig. 2 to Fig. 5 depict the true
and estimated values of the direction cosine in one trial. The
bottom panels in the ﬁgures plot the corresponding Bayesian
MSE (Bmse). We computed the average Bmse over 500
trials for each array. Fig. 6 plots the average Bmse. All four
arrays track the direction cosine reasonably well. As expected,
the full ULA exhibits the highest accuracy as it has the
lowest average Bmse. All sparse arrays exhibit degradation
in performance compared to the full ULA. Among the three
sparse arrays, the coprime array outperforms the other arrays.
The nested array shows the highest average MSE.
V. CONCLUSION
We investigated the efﬁcacy of an EKF for tracking the di-
rection cosine of a plane wave, modeled by an autoregressive
process of order 1. We applied the EKF to data collected from
a full array comprising 10 sensors and comparing its perfor-
mance against sparse arrays with an equivalent aperture but
comprising only 6 sensors. Speciﬁcally, we explored coprime,
nested, and SISA sparse arrays, discerning varying levels
of performance across these conﬁgurations. Comparison of
the average MSEs revealed that the full ULA outperforms
